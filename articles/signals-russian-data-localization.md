In the span of ten days, Ukrainian drones struck five Russian data centers
associated with Yandex, Sberbank, and VKontakte, disrupting services used for
communications, navigation, banking, and government functions. The attacks
exposed a strategic tension in Russia’s digital policy: keeping data and
computing within national borders can increase state control, but concentrating
critical infrastructure can also make it more vulnerable to physical attack.

Beginning October 8, strikes hit three data centers in the Ryazan, Kaluga, and
Vladimir regions. They affected facilities operated by Yandex, Russia’s largest
technology and internet company, and disrupted services including Yandex Mail,
Pay, and Cloud. A facility in Sasovo housed two of Yandex’s three
supercomputers, including one ranked among the world’s most powerful. Those
systems rely on U.S.-designed Nvidia A100 chips, which could be difficult to
replace amid export restrictions and sanctions. [Reporting on the
attacks](https://apnews.com/article/russia-ukraine-war-data-yandex-ai-digital-2886cb306264b9429bd317bde4bf01fa)
has highlighted their potential effects on both everyday services and Russia’s
AI ambitions.

Further strikes hit a Sberbank data center in Balakovo on October 14 and a
VKontakte facility in the Leningrad region four days later. The attacks also
followed Russian strikes on Ukrainian data centers and telecommunications
infrastructure. As data centers become targets in the war, the ability to keep
essential services running when individual facilities are damaged is a matter of
national resilience.

Ukraine took steps toward that kind of redundancy in 2022, moving critical
government and private-sector data to servers in allied European countries
through U.S. cloud providers. [Microsoft’s account of Ukraine’s wartime
technology response](https://news.microsoft.com/en-cee/2023/01/20/how-technology-helped-ukraine-resist-during-wartime/)
describes the role of cloud infrastructure in helping services withstand
attacks. This approach can reduce dependence on any one domestic facility, but
it also creates reliance on foreign companies. Starlink’s restrictions in
Ukraine have shown how decisions by a private provider can affect operations
that depend on its services.

Russia has pursued a different model. Since the 2019 Sovereign Internet Law,
Moscow has expanded state authority over the country’s internet infrastructure.
Its data-localization rules also require personal data collected about Russian
civilians to be stored on Russian soil. These policies support a more
state-controlled internet, sometimes described as a “splinternet,” separated
from the global network. But localization alone does not create physical
insecurity: the risk grows when data and computing capacity are concentrated
and systems have few alternatives if facilities are disrupted. [Analysis of
Russia’s internet isolation](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/in-depth-research-reports/issue-brief/reassessing-runet-russian-internet-isolation-and-implications-for-russian-cyber-behavior/)
describes the broader policy and security consequences.

The choice is not simply between digital independence and dependence on foreign
providers. Distributed infrastructure can improve resilience while creating
new dependencies; centralized infrastructure can strengthen domestic control
while creating critical chokepoints. As Europe pursues greater technological
sovereignty, governments will face the same balancing act. [The European
Commission’s digital sovereignty agenda](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/strengthening-europes-tech-sovereignty-2026-06-03_en)
raises a question that extends well beyond Russia: sovereignty depends not only
on where data is stored, but also on whether critical services can recover when
their infrastructure is attacked.
