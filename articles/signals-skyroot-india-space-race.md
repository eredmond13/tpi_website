On July 18, Indian startup Skyroot Aerospace launched its Vikram-I rocket from
the Satish Dhawan Space Centre in Sriharikota, marking the
[first successful orbital launch by an Indian private company](https://www.reuters.com/science/indias-skyroot-launches-vikram-1-first-private-orbital-rocket-mission-2026-07-18/).
The flight, part of Mission Aagaman, was designed to collect in-flight
performance data for the rocket.
Coming amid renewed global interest in commercial space, the launch also
demonstrates how public infrastructure, regulation, and private investment can
combine to build a national space industry.

Skyroot was founded in Hyderabad in 2018 by former Indian Space Research
Organisation (ISRO) scientists
[Pawan Kumar Chandana and Naga Bharath Daka](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/tabloid/hyderabad-chronicle/indias-rocket-boys-1973060).
The two met after joining ISRO in 2012, where Daka worked on avionics and launch
vehicle systems and Chandana on propulsion. They left the agency to pursue a
private company with the goal of making space more accessible. Their decision
coincided with a shift in Indian space policy: the government established the
Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Center (IN-SPACe) in 2020 to
promote and authorize commercial space activity. Skyroot’s rocket launched
from an ISRO-owned and operated spaceport, while the company retained
independence over its development, investment, and strategic direction.

Private capital has helped fund that development. In May 2026, Skyroot
[raised $60 million](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/skyroot-aerospace-raises-60-mn-becomes-a-unicorn-valued-at-1-1-bn-126050700238_1.html)
in a round co-led by Singapore’s sovereign wealth fund GIC and Sherpalo
Ventures. The investment valued the company at more than $1.1 billion, making
it [India’s first space-tech unicorn](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/deep-dive/is-skyroots-unicorn-milestone-indian-space-techs-coming-of-age-moment/2993921/1).
BlackRock, Playbook Partners, and the Shanghvi family office also participated,
bringing together institutional, venture, and family investment to support
manufacturing and work on the next rocket in the Vikram series.

Vikram-I can carry 350 kilograms to low Earth orbit. Its design combines a
carbon-composite structure, solid-fuel boosters, and a 3D-printed liquid
engine. With its flight, India became the third country, after the United
States and China, where a private company has independently achieved orbital
launch capability. The commercial sector is also growing beyond one company:
India’s Department of Space
[reported 399 space startups](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2220433&reg=48&lang=2)
and estimated the sector’s value at $8.4 billion, with significant growth
expected over the next decade.

Skyroot’s milestone is therefore not simply a story of a rocket reaching
orbit. It illustrates how governments can shape strategic industries by
providing shared infrastructure, creating regulatory pathways, and enabling
private firms to attract capital and pursue their own development. That model
also raises policy questions about how public agencies oversee commercial
activity and how national capabilities develop alongside private investment.
As space becomes more central to economic and geopolitical competition, the
ability to align public support with private innovation may help determine
which countries can build and sustain a competitive space sector.
