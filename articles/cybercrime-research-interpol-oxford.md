---
title: "Junior Fellow advances cybercrime research with INTERPOL and the University of Oxford"
date: 2025-09-15
category: News
summary: Cynthia Tan spent nine weeks at Oxford mapping how law enforcement agencies collaborate on cybercrime, and found cryptocurrency in nearly three-quarters of U.S. financial cybercrime indictments since 2020.
---

Junior Fellow Cynthia Tan travelled to the United Kingdom this summer as a Visiting Scholar at
Oxford's Department of Sociology. Over nine weeks she built a social network analysis mapping how
law enforcement agencies collaborate on cybercrime cases, drawing on U.S. Department of Justice
financial cybercrime indictments to trace the roles of national and international bodies and the use
of cryptocurrency as an anonymising tool. The project sets out both where cooperation is strong and
where gaps persist in international law between agencies.

Much of the groundwork came from her earlier role as a research assistant on the institute's Bitcoin
and Financial Freedom study, which examined how offenders exploit cryptocurrency in financial
schemes. At Oxford she extended that work, and found that roughly one third of U.S. Department of
Justice financial cybercrime indictments from 2020 to 2025 involved Bitcoin specifically, with
nearly three quarters involving cryptocurrency more broadly. She plans to continue examining the
structural role of cryptocurrency in transnational cybercrime and what it implies for comparative
approaches to international law and cooperation.

Over the same period she joined INTERPOL's Academic Cyber Offender Prevention Network and attended
the Fifth InterCOP Conference at INTERPOL headquarters in Lyon, meeting global law enforcement
leaders and researchers working on cybercrime prevention.

Tan has approached prevention from another angle as well. As a Laidlaw Scholar she worked with an
FBI Special Agent studying how to deter youth involvement in cybercrime, a project that underlined
the value of early intervention. Together these experiences gave her the technical and policy lens
that shaped the Oxford research.

Her work at Oxford was supported by the Department of Sociology, whose guidance helped refine the
scope of the project, and by the Frederic Conger Wood Fellowship, awarded by the Institute for
European Studies, part of the Einaudi Center for International Studies.
